Significance: 
The Army Reactors Experimental Area was built for 
the United States Army to test reactor concepts 
suitable for mobile power plants. The objective 
was to develop a family of nuclear power plants in 
a range of kilowatt capacity configurations. Some 
would be stationary; others, mobile. They were 
intended to replace diesel-powered plants in 
isolated or challenging locations where fuel 
supply lines were costly to maintain. 
Between 1957 and 1965, three test reactors 
operated: SL-1, GCRE, and ML-1. The SL-1 was a 
boiling water concept, used to train military 
crews in reactor operations. The GCRE was a gas-
cooled concept, expected to reduce the complexity, 
size, and weight of the power plant by eliminating 
apparatus required for water-cooled heat transfer 
systems. ML-1 was a gas-cooled field unit. 
The SL-1 was destroyed in a 1961 accident 
that killed three men. The accident remains the 
only reactor explosion resulting in the direct 
loss of human life in the United States. 
The Army canceled the program in 1965, partly 
because the high cost of building and operating 
the plants resulted in few orders. Expenditures 
for the Vietnam War mounted in the 1960s, and war 
priorities supplanted the funding for the Army's 
developmental program. Outside of Idaho, the Army 
operated five plants in isolated locations. 
After the Army cancellation, the Department 
of Energy {and its successor agencies) adapted the 
facilities for other research purposes.

PART ONE 
INTRODUCTION TO THE IDAHO NATIONAL ENGINEERING 
AND ENVIRONMENTAL LABORATORY 
The Atomic Energy Commission (AEC} established the National 
Reactor Testing Station (NRTS) in 1949 as a place for the safe 
development of nuclear energy. It selected the desert site in 
eastern Idaho on the Snake River Plain for its abundant supply of 
subsurface water and its relative isolation from densely 
populated settlements. The land already was in public ownership 
because the United States Navy had used it as a proving ground in 
connection with its Pocatello Ordnance Depot during World War II. 
The NRTS presently consists of about 890 square miles. Its 
name was changed to Idaho National Engineering Laboratory in 1974 
and then to Idaho National Engineering and Environmental 
Laboratory in 1997. The business of the NRTS was to experiment 
with and then accumulate and disseminate knowledge about nuclear 
reactors. One of the major goals of the United States Congress 
was to promote a commercial nuclear power industry. Much of the 
testing and experimentation at the NRTS was related to reactor 
safety and promoted this goal directly. Military applications, 
although focused on weapon systems such as nuclear-powered 
submarines and aircraft bombers, were expected to generate 
knowledge and experience transferable to a commercial industry. 
The AEC hired a private contractor to operate the central 
management functions of the testing station, while subcontractors 
built individual reactors and related experiments and projects. 
By 1957, when construction began for the Army's first reactor 
experiments, central services such as fire protection, electrical 
service, laboratory support, library, records management, health 
physics support, bus service, and others were well established. 
Scattered across the site are a series of intense activity 
centers where experiments took place or where operations continue 
today. The centers are connected by roads, railroads, and utility 
lines. From the air, these activity centers appear as small,

This Historic American Engineering Record (HAER) is being

waste sites and otherwise ''cleaning up" the environment that was 
compromised by nuclear testing activities.

initials "ARA'' followed by the building's number, as in ARA-608.

ARA-II, and ARA-III. For the sake of appropriate historical

PART TWO 
ORIGIN OF THE ARMY REACTORS PROGRAM AT NRTS: 1957-1965 
In the 1950s (and today}, the conventional method of 
supplying electricity to any isolated United States Army base or 
mobile field station was to transport a diesel generator to the 
site and then operate a supply line from the nearest depot to 
keep the station supplied with operating fuel. Trucking or flying 
the fuel to bases such as those at Arctic locations where road 
access was impossible or flying weather-restricted, was 
difficult, dangerous, and costly. 
After World War II, the possibilities of atomic power 
tempted the Army as they did the U.S. Navy and U.S. Air Force. 
That a tiny handful of nuclear fuel could replace the logistical 
headache of fuel transport to remote locations was an idea hard 
to resist. A nuclear power plant might be mobile, able to move 
with a field hospital or command center. Perhaps it could be 
portable, mounted on a barge and towable from one port to another 
as needed. Reactors might be small, medium, and large, varying in 
capacity for a wide range of applications. The power plants only 
needed to be small enough, light-weight enough, and cheap enough. 
The Army's nuclear program aimed to meet these three challenges. 
The Army organized an Office of Research and Development in 
1951 to begin a program. Its chief, General K.D. Nichols, thought 
that the Army's efforts, in addition to supplying the Army with a 
useful power plant alternative, might help to speed up the 
ultimate development of a commercial industry. He and others 
often used this argument as they enlisted support for the idea. 
The Army placed the Nuclear Development program under the 
supervision of the United States Army Corps of Engineers. 2 
Meeting initial resistance from the AEC staff, which desired 
to retain an exclusive initiative in developing a commercial 
industry, the Army gradually acquired allies in Alvin Weinberg, 
director of the Oak Ridge National Laboratory; and Admiral Lewis

The Army intended to develop a family of three basic types 
of power plants. A stationary plant would be a permanent 
installation that could serve a base in a remote area otherwise 
difficult to supply with fuel. It would not be designed for 
relocation elsewhere. A portable power plant would be pre-
assembled for rapid erection in the field. A limited number of 
"packages" would make up the plant, each of which could fit in an 
air cargo transport or truck. The plant could be disassembled and 
then relocated to another site. A mobile power plant could move 
intact from one site to another without being broken down and 
reassembled at all--possibly operate even while being moved. 4 
Further refining its goals, the Army selected operating 
ranges for its nuclear plants. A "low power" reactor would 
produce in the range of 100 to 1,000 kilowatts. "Medium power" 
reactors would supply from 1,000 to 10,000 kilowatts, and "high 
power" facilities could range between 10 to 40 megawatts. 5 
The Army institutionalized these concepts in the names of 
its prototypes and experiments. Its first prototype, which went 
on line at Fort Belvoir, thus carried the designation SM-1, a 
"Stationary Medium Power" reactor. In its early enthusiasm, the 
Army planned to build seventeen different projects. Not all of 
them materialized. Seven projects went into service, seven others 
were designed and not completed, and three were experiments built 
at the National Reactor Testing Station in Idaho. 6

PART THREE 
THE ARMY COMES TO THE NATIONAL REACTOR TESTING STATION 
The Fort Belvoir reactor, within eighteen miles of The White 
House, was a pressurized water reactor, the same concept that 
U.S. Navy Admiral Hyman Rickover had installed in Nautilus, the 
submarine. Although other reactor concepts promised to embody 
virtues of light weight and simplicity so eagerly sought by the 
Army, pressurized water technology was the proven state of the 
art at the time. In April 1957, in a ceremony designed to 
symbolize its potential for both civilian and military 
applications, Fort Belvoir's first nuclear-generat7d electricity 
powered both a printing press and a radar antenna. 
Reactors cooled with pressurized water had several 
disadvantages. The coolant circulated in a primary loop through 
the reactor and collected heat. The heat was transferred to water 
in a secondary loop. This loop transferred heat to a boiler, 
which produced steam to run a turbine/generator set. The coolant 
piping, pumps, valves, controls, and instrumentation added 
considerable weight, bulk, and complexity to the total outfit. 
The Army, therefore, set out to experiment with two 
alternative reactor concepts. The first was a boiling water 
reactor. In this design, ordinary water boils as it passes 
through the hot reactor core. The steam powers the turbine. The 
system eliminates the secondary loop and the heat exchanger 
equipment. The Army and the AEC engaged Argonne National 
Laboratory to design a stationary reactor in the "low" power 
range that might be suitable for a remote location. The Army had 
the DEW Line (Defense Early Warning, later the Ballistic Missile 
Early Warning System) in mind. The DEW Line consisted of dozens 
of manned radar stations ringing the Arctic Circle on the watch 
for Soviet air invasion. The Army wanted the plant small enough 
to haul on a 30-ton trailer. The prototype was named SL-1, and it 
was built on the NRTS at the Army Reactors Experimental Area. 8 
The second alternative was a "gas-cooled" reactor, the 
experiments for which were named "GCRE." In this concept, a gas

PART FOUR

The SL-1 was the first experiment ready to build. In August 
1955, the AEC chose Pioneer Services and Engineering Company of 
Chicago as the architect/engineer. Bid requests began to go out

terrain. Each new experiment had to meet siting criteria 
administered by a Site Selection Committee at the NRTS and 
approved by the AEC in Washington. The Committee knew from the 
outset that the Army program would consist of three experiments. 
It directed that the site be placed a few miles west of Argonne

PART FIVE 
DESCRIPTIONS OF MAJOR ARA FACILITIES 
Like all of the other buildings at the NRTS, the ARA 
buildings were subject to AEC design guidelines and standards. 
These policies required buildings to be economical and to use 
standard materials. After a building or group of buildings was 
designed, construction contractors bid for the jobs, the low 
bidder typically winning the work. 
As a result of these practices, ARA buildings resembled the 
vast majority of buildings clustered at the NRTS. Most of them 
were rectangular in shape. Their walls were made of concrete, 
pumice block, or metal. Wood siding was very scarce at all ARA

ARA-III: GCRE Reactor Operating Area 
The second experimental reactor concept for a gas-cooled 
nuclear power plant required substantially different kinds of 
facilities than the SL-1. Instead of using water to carry heat 
away from the nuclear reaction, this concept required that a gas 
(nitrogen) pick up and transfer the heat to a steam boiler that 
would drive a turbine/generator set. However, a turbine/generator 
was not to be part of the experiment, so heat had to be wasted 
directly to the environment. The gas-cooled concept had 
implications for the construction of the reactor building, the 
cooling apparatus, and the volume of contaminated water that 
would have to be stored at the site. In addition, the gas-cooled 
reactor was preparatory to a very small and mobile reactor. The 
main building and the site were designed with future tests of a 
mobile prototype, the ML-1, in mind. (See Figure 7, page 24.)

SL-1 went critical for the first time on August 11, 1958, 
and produced electricity two months later on October 24. It was 
the first power plant reactor in the country to use aluminum-clad 
fuel elements, which heretofore had been used only in test 
reactors like the MTR. A new alloy overcame the low melting point 
of aluminum. After SL-1, aluminum alloys were used widely. 21 
The reactor operated fairly routinely after its successful 
first run of 500 hours. The AEC contracted Combustion Engineering 
Company to operate the plant and train future crews. The purpose 
of the reactor was to accumulate and record operating data on 
core burn-up, test proposed components for future reactors, and 
make improvements in the design and operation of the plant. 
Several cadres of trainees were certified. 
On December 23, 1960, the operators shut down SL-1 for 
routine maintenance. Chores included calibrating instruments and 
installing flux wires in the reactor core. On January 3, 1961, 
three military crew members on an evening shift were winding up 
the maintenance work and reassembling the reactor in preparation 
for another run that would begin the next day. 
At 9:01 pm a violent explosion occurred in the reactor 
vessel, killing all three men. This was the first fatal accident 
in the AEC's history of reactor operations. 
The events of the next few days saw a mobilization of effort 
on several levels of operation. The first necessity was to remove 
the bodies of the men from the reactor building. One of the men 
was relatively easily accessible and was removed the night of the 
accident still alive. He died on the way to a dispensary. Teams 
working in relays, fully dressed in protective clothing and full-
face respirators, and allowed to remain in the reactor building 
for only seconds at a time, managed to retrieve the second man 
later on January 4.22

PART SEVEN 
THE END OF THE ARMY REACTORS PROGRAM 
In view of the continuing difficulty finding missions for 
their small reactors--and the continuing difficulty in keeping 
the ML-1 from breaking down--the Army and the AEC concluded that 
the ML-1 program might eventually achieve its objectives, but 
that it would cost too much to achieve. Nuclear plants, 
particularly in the low-power end of the spectrum, could not 
compete with diesel plants: Using the Army's Antarctica reactor 
as an example, the initial cost of the nuclear plant was $6-7 
million; for diesel, $350,000. A nuclear plant required a crew of 
20 highly trained men; a diesel plant, six. 
Partly explaining the Army's reluctance to continue 
financing nuclear experiments was the nation's growing 
involvement in the Vietnam War. The Department of Defense needed 
funds to prosecute the war. First the AEC and then the Army 
phased out the funding for the ML-1 development program by June 
1966. 41 This action effectively ended the involvement of the 
NRTS in the Army's nuclear development program. 
An Army Ad Hoc Study Group took up the question of the rest 
of its program in 1969. One of the participants summed up the 
situation by saying, "Nuclear power is a solution in search of a 
problem." In the end, the group decided that it was only in 
selected remote situations that nuclear systems were cost-
competitive with conventional diesel plants. Basically, no 
military requirements really existed for nuclear power, but may

PART NINE 
THE SIGNIFICANCE OF THE ARMY REACTORS PROGRAM 
The Army Reactors Program grew out of an idea that derived 
in great part from the nuclear enthusiasm and nuclear naivete of 
the post-World War II world. From the vantage of the turn of the 
20th Century, it also appears that the idea simply may have 
arisen ahead of its time. 
The naivete that beguiled the Army and everyone else who 
hoped that fissioning atoms could be used for the peaceful 
generation of electrical power was that it was only a matter of 
study, application, financing, and engineering before problems 
would be solved and unit costs for small-scale units would come 
down. Unit costs for large-scale nuclear power plants did, in 
fact, come down, and nuclear power became competitive with 
conventional power generation in some parts of the United States. 
However, the economies of scale did not materialize for the 
Army's miniature plants, which failed to compete with the

intelligence, training, practice, and preparation as the crew of 
a huge commercial plant. All of this was costly. 
After nearly half a century of effort, some problems have 
not been solved. The by-products of nuclear power generation--
from small or large plants--are spent fuel elements and other 
hazardous and radioactive wastes, both liquid and solid. The 
complex political, technological, social, and economic 
arrangements required to cope with these dangerous materials have 
still, after a half century of development, not stabilized. 
Therefore, even after the Army produced and proved a 
reasonably credible product, that product was unable to compete 
economically with the conventional diesel-powered generator. The 
diesel generator could be mobilized quickly; it operated with far 
less training and much less risk upon failure. 
At the NRTS, the joint Army/AEC program represented a set of 
experiments with alternative nuclear reactor concepts. As the 
1960s approached, it appeared that the pressurized water reactor 
would dominate the commercial power industry. The gas-cooled 
concept offered a potentially more efficient power plant, but in 
the face of an overwhelming national trend favoring another 
concept, interest in developing and perfecting it weakened. 
Still, the experiments produced knowledge. Analytical 
procedures, prediction of reactor behavior, testing of alloys in 
fuel elements and components, evaluation of fuel element 
performance and lifetime--the operation of the SL-1 and the GCRE 
enhanced all of these. The final disassembly of the ML-1, 
however, suggested that the state of materials science was not 
yet up to the operating heat of the gas-cooled reactor. Its many 
failures resulted primarily from the failure of metal and of 
welds to hold up to protracted corrosive exposure to high heat. 
The idea of the gas-cooled reactor for a small power plant was 
too advanced for the state of materials science at the time.
